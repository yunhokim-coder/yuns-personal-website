Master’s thesis · Kwangwoon University · 2024

Can Grit Be Coached?

A seven-session group coaching program built on a culturally informed, three-factor model of grit—tested with upper-elementary students against a two-factor program and a no-program control group.

7weekly 50-minute sessions
24fifth- and sixth-grade students
3groups compared
3measurement points
01 / The question

Grit, read in a Korean classroom

Grit is usually defined by two factors: perseverance of effort and consistency of interest toward long-term goals. That model was built largely in individualist cultural settings. In more interdependent contexts such as Korea, goals are often shaped with families and teachers, and staying on course can mean adjusting a sub-goal rather than holding every plan fixed.

Datu and colleagues (2017) proposed a Triarchic Model of Grit that adds a third factor, adaptability to situations. To my knowledge, no intervention had yet tried to strengthen that third factor. My thesis set out to build one for upper-elementary students, at an age when schoolwork grows heavier and staying engaged starts to matter more.

02 / The design

Change only what is being tested

Sixteen fifth-graders at a Seoul youth center were randomly assigned to two groups of eight; eight students at a psychological center in Gimpo formed the control group. Grit (a Korean adaptation of the Triarchic Model of Grit Scale, 11 items, α = .826) and self-direction (a 10-item proactive-personality measure) were assessed before, right after, and two weeks after the program.

Experimental · n = 8Three-factor program, including two adaptability sessions
Comparison · n = 8Two-factor program: identical except sessions 5–6, which used self-control and mindfulness activities
Control · n = 8No program
Program material

Making grit make sense to a ten-year-old

Grit is an abstract idea, so the program introduced it through a story. I wrote the storyboard for a 20-panel webtoon, Rit and the School Play, and commissioned an illustrator to draw it. Rit is cast as a villager instead of the lead, hears a little devil say “no talent,” keeps practicing anyway, and earns the lead in the next play. Only then does the comic name what happened: grit, and the idea that effort, like learning an instrument, grows with practice. The webtoon opened the first session.

Storyboard by Yunho Kim; illustrations by a commissioned artist. Panels 14–15 and 19 include an illustrated representation of grit researcher Angela Duckworth, created for teaching.

03 / Seven sessions

Seven sessions, each built on the GROW model

Following a systematic program-development model and a pilot run, each session moved through the GROW coaching sequence: Goal, Reality, Options, and Will. Short quizzes, videos, and small rewards kept attention high, and a weekly grit journal carried each session into the week that followed.

  1. 1
    Understanding gritMeet the new transfer student, Grit!

    Ground rules, a grit webtoon, an O/X quiz, a first grit self-check, and a weekly grit-tracking journal.

  2. 2
    Grit mindset · optimismHakuna Matata!

    An optimism animation, body-language charades, and a “worry jar” that names the worries keeping students from trying.

  3. 3
    Grit mindset · growthMake our brains stronger!

    Opening the worry jar to see which worries still matter, then rewriting fixed-mindset phrases in growth-mindset language.

  4. 4
    Grit behavior · effortPlan it smart

    Brainstorming action ideas and turning a grit goal into a SMART plan.

  5. 5
    Overcoming barriers · adaptabilityWhat color are my glasses?

    “Same situation, different thoughts”: a folk tale about a worried mother of two merchants, a letter to a struggling peer, and a short role play on reframing a problem.

  6. 6
    Overcoming barriers · adaptabilityI’m a problem solver!

    Sorting green, yellow, and red solutions, then building and trying out a personal problem-solving plan.

  7. 7
    Sustaining gritGrit is my friend!

    What a coach does, a clip from Facing the Giants, a grit calligraphy frame, and a commitment to self-coaching.

Sessions 5–6 target adaptability through social problem-solving. They are the only sessions that differ between the experimental and comparison programs.

The experimental group’s grit rose significantly from pretest to posttest (t = −4.57, p < .01) and remained significantly above pretest two weeks later (t = −3.53, p < .01). Its pre–post gain was also significantly larger than the comparison group’s (t = 2.65, p < .05).

Self-direction ≈

Self-direction rose slightly in the experimental group, but no main or interaction effect was significant. Proactive self-direction may still be emerging at this age, and a sample of 24 had limited power to detect a small effect.

05 / What it adds, and what it cannot yet say

A first step toward culturally informed grit interventions

To my knowledge, this was the first grit program built on the triarchic model, and the first elementary grit program structured around GROW coaching. Because the experimental and comparison programs differed only in sessions 5–6, the study offers an early comparison of adaptability-focused activities against self-control and mindfulness activities. In this sample, the adaptability version produced the larger change in grit.

The work later became my oral presentation at the Korean Coaching Psychology Association’s 2024 winter conference.

Reading the results carefully
  • Small sample, drawn only from Seoul and Gyeonggi.
  • The control group came from a different site and included sixth-graders.
  • Grit was measured by self-report only.
  • A two-week follow-up is too short to show whether new habits last.
  • I delivered the experimental program myself.

Qualitative research · Pet loss · 2022–2025

Listening Before Designing

Before building support for people grieving a companion animal, we asked the people who had been through it what the loss changed, what the animal had been to them, and what actually helped.

  1. 01Literature reviewGrief, attachment, and coping after pet loss
  2. 02Semi-structured interviewsOwners who had experienced and recovered from the loss
  3. 03Structured individual and focus group interviewsNarrowing open accounts into concrete support needs
  4. 04Needs-based programA memorial-night program and an ISBN-registered eBook
01 / Why this question

A real grief, without the rituals

Losing a companion animal can bring grief comparable to losing a person. In a study of 211 college students who had lost a pet and 146 who had lost a person, grief after human loss was only slightly higher (d ≈ 0.28–0.30), and in both groups closeness to the one who died was the strongest predictor of how deep the grief ran (Eckerd et al., 2016).

Yet pet loss has few of the social rituals that help people mourn a human death, and many owners are left to grieve without being asked about it. I wanted support built from what bereaved owners themselves described, not from what we assumed they needed.

02 / How we listened

From open stories to specific needs

As supervising counselor and researcher, I led a team of three research assistants under the supervision of a Ph.D.-level clinical psychologist. We began with the literature on grief, attachment, and coping, then held semi-structured interviews with people who had experienced and recovered from pet loss.

The open interviews showed us what mattered; structured individual and focus group interviews then narrowed those accounts into specific support needs that a program could answer.

03 / What we heard

Three things owners told us

The absence fills the house
“The house felt unusually quiet, and the empty space felt huge.”
“I would call their name without thinking, then realize. I couldn’t even put the food bowl away.”
It was family
“A being who gave me unconditional love and comfort.”
“My other half in the family, my best friend.”
“A friend who needed no words.”
People invent their own rituals
“I wrote a letter.”
“I made a stone of my dog and kept it on my dresser.”
“My family and I talked about the dog we had let go.”

Quotes are translated from Korean and shared without identifying details.

04 / From findings to support

Giving grief a form people had already been reaching for

What stood out most was the third theme. Without established rituals, owners were improvising their own: letters, photos set to music, keepsakes, family conversations. The program built on that instead of replacing it. Its memorial-night sessions paired psychoeducation with making small figures and memory pieces of the animal, and with time to share memories with others.

Because grief also differs with attachment style, the resulting eBook, Memorial Night: Pet Loss Syndrome (2025), describes how secure, anxious, avoidant, and disorganized attachment tend to shape reactions to loss and which coping approaches may fit each, alongside recovery practices that help across styles.

Undergraduate psychometric research · 2019

From an Idea to a Measure

Developing the Happiness Habit Scale — and tracing how a student research project later informed an applied assessment portfolio.

26initial items
163young-adult participants
15final items
3factors
01 / Building the scale

Turning an idea about everyday happiness into measurable items

Working in a four-member undergraduate research team, I co-developed an initial 26-item pool intended to capture happiness-related habits. Data from 163 young adults were analyzed in SPSS 20.0 using item analysis, internal-consistency analysis, and exploratory factor analysis.

The initial 26-item measure demonstrated Cronbach’s α = .808. Criterion-related validity was examined through its relationship with the Satisfaction With Life Scale, showing a significant positive association (r = .399, p < .001).

02 / What emerged

A 15-item, three-factor measure

The scale-development process resulted in a 15-item measure organized around three factors. Rather than treating happiness as a single feeling, the final structure captured patterns related to acting intentionally, interpreting adversity, and relating to other people.

02Positive Perception of Adversity
03Interpersonal Relationships
03 / From research to practice

From psychometric study to an applied assessment portfolio

Following the scale-development work, Sky-Dream Coaching Psychology Center designed a physical cover and response sheet around the Happiness Habit Scale. A separate projective happiness activity was also incorporated as a complementary qualitative component, bringing the materials together as an applied assessment portfolio.

This later portfolio illustrates how a research product can move into practice while still requiring a clear distinction between what was psychometrically examined and what was added for exploratory use.

Methodological distinction Only the self-report Happiness Habit Scale underwent the reliability, factor-analytic, and criterion-related validity analyses reported in the 2019 study. The projective component is presented here as a complementary exploratory activity and is not described as a separately standardized or validated psychological test.

Undergraduate experimental research · 2019

Can Taking Their Perspective Change Our Attitudes?

A 2 × 2 experiment on perspective-taking, attribution, and multicultural acceptance among young adults, and what a null result taught me about designing materials people can actually follow.

108young-adult participants
2 × 2conditions + control
87responses analyzed
1significant effect: gender
01 / Why this question

Programs taught newcomers to adapt. Few asked the rest of us to change.

By the end of 2017, Korea’s foreign resident population had reached 2.18 million, 4.2% of the registered population, and the number of multicultural students was rising every year even as total enrollment fell. Yet Korean adolescents ranked 21st of 38 countries in multicultural acceptance.

Most multicultural education in Korea focused on helping immigrants and their children adjust, and very few programs targeted university students. Psychological research on what shifts the attitudes of the majority group, rather than how people who face discrimination cope, was also thin. Our four-member team asked whether two psychological levers could move those attitudes: taking the perspective of a person who faces discrimination, and how we explain why the discrimination happened.

02 / The design

Two manipulations, one outcome

We crossed two factors in a 2 × 2 between-subjects experiment. Perspective-taking varied whether participants approached a discrimination scenario from the position of the person experiencing it or as an observer. Attribution varied whether they explained the conflict through internal or external causes. A control group received no manipulation. The outcome was a Rasch-based multicultural acceptance scale developed for university students (Park, 2013).

RRandom assignment after gender-stratified sampling
×Posttest-only control group design, so a pretest could not signal the hypothesis
03 / What we found

Flat across conditions, apart by gender

Multicultural acceptance averaged 3.96 (SD = 0.38) on a 5-point scale. A two-way ANOVA in SPSS found no main effect of perspective-taking, no main effect of attribution, and no interaction. Women, however, scored higher than men regardless of condition, consistent with earlier studies.

Means are read from the figures in our research report and rounded, so treat them as approximate. The axis starts at 3.6 to make small differences visible.

04 / What a null result taught me

If participants can’t follow the task, the manipulation never happens

In our report, we looked for why the manipulations did nothing. Twenty-one of the 108 responses had to be excluded, largely because participants misread the task, a sign that our materials were harder to follow than we had assumed. A single scenario also struggled to stand in for the many dimensions the acceptance scale covers, from nationality and race to religion.

The study did not show that perspective-taking or attribution change attitudes. What it showed me is that an intervention is only as strong as participants’ understanding of it: test materials with the people they are for before measuring their effect.

About this study Unpublished undergraduate research report, Kwangwoon University (2019), by a four-member team. The research question was my idea; we designed and ran the experiment, analyzed the data, and wrote the report together. Results are reported as found, including the null effects.
